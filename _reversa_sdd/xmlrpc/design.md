## Main Flow

1. **layered enablement resolution** — pre_option_enable_xmlrpc, then option_enable_xmlrpc defaulting to true, then the xmlrpc_enabled filter; the chain exists to respect back-compat filters written against the removed option.
   Source: `wp-includes/class-wp-xmlrpc-server.php:185` 🟢
2. **per-call credential authentication** — Each method receives username and password as parameters and authenticates independently, so credentials traverse the wire on every request with no built-in rate limit.
   Source: `wp-includes/class-wp-xmlrpc-server.php` 🟢

## Alternative Flows

- **BR-XR-01:** XML-RPC is enabled by default; disabling requires add_filter('xmlrpc_enabled', '__return_false'). 🟢
- **BR-XR-02:** The enable_xmlrpc option is deprecated since 3.5.0; its pre_option and option filters are consulted only for back-compat. 🟢

## Risks and Gaps

- 🟢 **F-XR-01** — A 211 KB always-on endpoint accepting credentials on every call is the largest legacy attack surface in WordPress; four of its six method families serve clients that no longer exist.
- 🟢 **F-XR-02** — Per-call credentials with no rate limiting make xmlrpc.php the canonical brute-force target; core supplies no throttle, exactly as with comment flooding.
- 🟢 **F-XR-03** — pingback.ping is a server-side fetch of an attacker-supplied URL, structurally identical to the oEmbed discovery risk but reachable unauthenticated.
- 🟢 **F-XR-04** — REST duplicates nearly all of wp.*; two complete remote APIs are maintained in parallel, one with schemas and permission callbacks and one without.
